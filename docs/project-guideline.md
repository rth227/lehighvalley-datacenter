# Data Center Impact on Bethlehem
Human-Centered AI Community Project | Project Guideline
1. Project overview
Data Center Impact on Bethlehem is a community-focused project exploring proposed data center development in Bethlehem and the surrounding Lehigh Valley. The goal is to create an informative, interactive website that helps residents understand what is being proposed, learn about potential impacts, hear different community perspectives, and participate in conversations about the area's future.

Rather than arguing for or against data centers, the project should present reliable information, document a range of community experiences, and help people make informed decisions.
The central question
How can communities navigate technological development while keeping people's needs, values, and voices at the center of the process?
Core objectives
- Inform: Explain data centers, how they work, why they are being proposed, and what is known about local projects.
- Investigate: Research possible environmental, economic, infrastructure, and quality-of-life impacts.
- Listen: Collect perspectives from residents, educators, researchers, community organizations, officials, and other relevant stakeholders.
- Connect: Help people understand the relationship between a complex technology and its potential local effects.
- Empower: Give residents accessible information and meaningful ways to participate in community discussions.
- Explore possibilities: Investigate ways technological development might address community needs and concerns.
2. The website
The website is the main deliverable. It should be easy to navigate, understandable to people without a technical background, visually engaging, and designed to encourage exploration rather than persuade visitors toward a predetermined conclusion.
Recommended website structure

1. Home page and project introduction

Introduce the project, explain why data centers are a local topic, and provide a clear path to explore proposals, potential impacts, and community voices.

2. Understanding data centers

Explain what data centers are, how they support cloud computing and AI, why companies build them, and what resources they use. Define technical terms in plain language.

3. Local proposals, map, and timeline

Document relevant proposed or approved projects, their locations, development status, public meetings, and major decisions. Distinguish confirmed facts from proposals and speculation.

4. Community voices and interviews

Feature short interview clips, quotes, and perspectives from residents, educators, community members, and other stakeholders. Include different viewpoints and explain who is speaking and in what capacity.

5. Potential impacts

Explore energy consumption, water use, environmental effects, employment, tax revenue, infrastructure, noise, land use, and quality of life. Explain what is known locally and what remains uncertain.

6. Community participation and future possibilities

Explain how residents can learn about proposals, attend meetings, ask questions, and share feedback. Explore possible ways to align development with community priorities.

7. Sources, methodology, and updates

Cite research, public records, meeting materials, and other evidence. Explain how interviews were conducted, how information was checked, and when the website was last updated.

These are recommended sections, not requirements to build seven separate pages. They can be combined into a smaller site if that makes the experience clearer.
3. Research and information gathering
The website should be grounded in research, not just general information about data centers. The most important distinction is between what is documented about local proposals, what research says about data centers in general, and what community members believe or experience.
A. Local development research
Investigate the relevant data center proposals in Bethlehem and the Lehigh Valley, including the development discussed in connection with Route 22.

For each relevant project, document:
- Project name, location, developer, and proposed size, when publicly available.
- Current status, such as proposed, under review, approved, under construction, or operational.
- Relevant zoning decisions, permits, public hearings, and planning documents.
- Known timelines and upcoming public meetings.
- Publicly documented commitments, projected benefits, and concerns.
Potential research sources include municipal websites, planning and zoning records, public meeting agendas and minutes, developer materials, local news coverage, and relevant regional agencies.

Important: Do not assume that a project is approved or will be built simply because it has been proposed or discussed publicly.
B. Potential impacts to investigate
Topic	Questions to explore
Energy	How much electricity might a facility require? What is known about grid capacity and additional demand?
Water	Will the facility use water for cooling? What are the projected demands and the local water implications?
Environment	What are the potential effects on emissions, land use, habitat, and local environmental conditions?
Economy	What construction and permanent jobs are projected? What tax revenue or other economic effects are documented?
Infrastructure	Could development affect roads, utilities, transmission infrastructure, or other public services?
Neighborhood quality of life	What concerns have been raised about noise, lighting, traffic, visual appearance, or nearby land uses?
Technology and AI	How do data centers support AI, cloud computing, and the digital services people use?
Community benefits	What opportunities might exist for local employment, education, infrastructure improvements, or other community priorities?

Avoid treating every possible impact as an established local fact. National research can help explain general risks and benefits, but local conclusions should rely on local evidence wherever possible.
C. Keep track of evidence
Maintain a research spreadsheet with columns such as:
- Topic or claim
- Project or geographic area
- Source and publication date
- Relevant quotation or finding
- Whether it is a confirmed fact, projection, opinion, or unresolved question
- Limitations or missing information
- Website section where it will appear
- Date last checked
This will make the website easier to maintain and help you identify areas where more research is needed.
4. Community interviews and outreach
Interviews are a major part of the project because they introduce the human side of a complicated technology issue.

The objective is not simply to collect opinions for or against data centers. It is to understand what people know, what they value, what concerns them, what opportunities they see, and what information they need.
A. Who to interview

Residents and community members

Explore their connection to Bethlehem, their awareness of local proposals, their concerns, and their hopes for the community.

Professors and educators

Explore technology literacy, public understanding, education, and how people can participate meaningfully in decisions about new infrastructure.

Local officials and community organizations

Ask about the decision-making process, public participation, local priorities, and where residents can find reliable information.

Technical experts, researchers, and industry representatives

Ask about how data centers work, resource requirements, potential benefits and drawbacks, and which questions require more local investigation.

You do not need to interview everyone in every category. Aim for a range of relevant perspectives, and be transparent about which voices are represented and which are missing.
B. Interviewing at community events and protests
Your attendance at community events and protests provides an opportunity to hear directly from people who are engaged with the issue.

Potential questions include:
- What brought you here today?
- What do you think people should understand about the proposed development?
- What questions or concerns do you have?
- Are there any potential benefits or opportunities you think should be considered?
- What information would help you form a more informed opinion?
- What would you like decision-makers to take into account?
Keep questions open-ended and non-leading. Do not assume that someone attending a protest opposes every form of data center development, or that someone who supports a project dismisses all concerns.
C. Interviewing Professor Pearl
Professor Pearl's interview can contribute a different perspective. Rather than asking her to evaluate the technical details of data center development, focus on her experience as a computer science professor, educator, and Bethlehem resident.

A suggested interview sequence:
1. Personal introduction: "Could you tell us a little about yourself and your experience living and working in Bethlehem? Are there things about the community that you particularly value?"
2. Background in technology: "What got you interested in computer science, and what excites you most about technology today?"
3. Technology literacy: "Your work brings together computer science and education. When new technologies are introduced into a community, how important do you think it is for people to understand how they work, and what can help make that information accessible to people with different backgrounds?"
4. Academia and public participation: "From your perspective as an educator, how can schools and universities help people better understand new technologies and participate in conversations about how those technologies might affect their communities?"
5. Closing reflection: "What would you hope the relationship between technological progress and local communities looks like in the future?"
Use follow-up questions when appropriate rather than forcing every planned question into the conversation.
D. Recording and publishing interviews
For each interview:
- Explain the purpose of the project and where the recording may appear.
- Obtain permission before recording and clarify consent for publishing video, audio, names, and quotations.
- Ask whether the participant prefers to be identified or remain anonymous, when anonymity is feasible.
- Capture a short introduction or enough context to identify the speaker's relevant perspective.
- Preserve the intended meaning when editing clips.
- Add captions and readable text for accessibility.
- Avoid editing a response in a way that misrepresents the participant.
Short clips are useful for the website, but the complete interview notes or recordings, when consent permits retaining them, can help preserve context and accuracy.
5. Neutrality, fairness, and responsible reporting
A central requirement is to make the website informative without turning it into an advocacy campaign.

Use these principles throughout the project:
- Separate fact from opinion. Label personal perspectives as personal perspectives.
- Use evidence for factual claims. Cite sources and distinguish projections from measured results.
- Represent competing considerations fairly. Discuss possible economic and technological benefits alongside environmental, infrastructure, and quality-of-life concerns.
- Do not manufacture balance. Give claims weight according to the quality of evidence, not simply the number of people expressing each position.
- Be transparent about uncertainty. If the water use, energy demand, tax implications, or employment effects of a specific proposal are unknown, say so.
- Avoid loaded language. Do not describe development as automatically beneficial, harmful, inevitable, or unnecessary.
- Protect participants. Respect consent, privacy, and the context in which interviews were collected.
- Correct errors. Make corrections when new evidence changes what the project can responsibly report.
The goal is not to make every visitor agree. It is to give visitors enough context to understand the issue and reach their own conclusions.
6. Human-Centered AI connection
Since this is a Human-Centered AI project, the website should do more than explain data centers. It should demonstrate how technology-related information can be presented in ways that respect and empower the people affected by it.

Consider the following design principles.
Accessibility and understanding

Explain technical concepts in plain language, define unfamiliar terms, use informative diagrams, and offer concise summaries.

Transparency and explainability

Show where information comes from, how conclusions were reached, and what is not yet known.

Participation and inclusion

Incorporate community voices, invite questions, and make the site useful to people with different educational backgrounds and levels of technical knowledge.

Fairness and human agency

Avoid steering visitors toward a predetermined opinion. Help them examine evidence and perspectives independently.

Iteration and responsiveness

Use community feedback to identify confusing explanations, missing perspectives, and opportunities to improve the site.

A useful way to frame the HCAI contribution is this: the project explores not only the impacts of technological infrastructure, but also how information and digital tools can help people participate in decisions about that infrastructure.

AI does not need to be added to the website simply to make it an AI project. A clear, trustworthy, participatory information experience can itself reflect human-centered design principles.

A feedback form should collect only the information needed for the project. Explain how submissions will be used, and do not publish identifying details without permission.
8. Suggested project workflow
1. Phase 1: Define the scope
   
   Finalize the project question, intended audience, geographic boundaries, and which local proposals will be covered. Establish the website's neutral framing.
2. Phase 2: Conduct background research
   
   Gather local planning documents, reliable news reports, technical research, and relevant public records. Start the research spreadsheet and identify gaps.
3. Phase 3: Conduct interviews and outreach
   
   Prepare interview guides, contact participants, attend relevant community events, collect consent, and organize recordings and notes.
4. Phase 4: Develop the website structure
   
   Create a sitemap, decide which information belongs in each section, sketch page layouts, and establish navigation and accessibility requirements.
5. Phase 5: Build the website
   
   Implement the main pages and reusable components. Add verified information, citations, interview clips, and interactive elements as they become ready.
6. Phase 6: Review for accuracy and fairness
   
   Verify claims, check project statuses, review interview context, test links, and make sure facts, estimates, and opinions are clearly distinguished.
7. Phase 7: Test with users
   
   Ask people unfamiliar with data centers to explore the website. Determine whether they understand the issue, can locate sources, and can distinguish confirmed information from unresolved questions.
8. Phase 8: Publish and maintain
   
   Publish the site, establish an update process, record the date of the latest review, and make corrections when new information becomes available.

10. How to measure success
The project should be evaluated by whether it helps people understand and engage with the issue, not by whether it changes their position on data centers.

Potential measures of success include:
- Visitors can explain what data centers do and why local development is being discussed.
- Visitors can find reliable sources and distinguish established facts from projections and opinions.
- The website includes a meaningful range of community perspectives.
- People can identify where proposals stand and where to find relevant public information.
- Users with different levels of technical knowledge can navigate the site successfully.
- Feedback identifies ways to improve the accuracy, clarity, inclusiveness, and usefulness of the information.
- The website can be updated as local developments and public discussions evolve.
The ultimate goal: create a trustworthy public resource that connects a complex technological issue to the people, places, and decisions that shape Bethlehem's future. The website should leave visitors better informed, more aware of different perspectives, and better equipped to participate in their community.
